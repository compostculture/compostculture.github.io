The case for the VermiHut Plus

The VermiHut is the more complete machine out of the box, and the listing says so with specifics rather than adjectives. Five trays are in the box and the stack is fixed at five — 50 liters of working capacity per the spec table, available on day one. The engineering details are the differentiator: a liquid collection tray molded into the base as one piece (no separate assembly, no leak points), a Worm Saver Tray that keeps worms out of the collection area while you drain tea, a lid system with a V-Board and coconut mat the listing credits for airflow and moisture balance, and base feet designed to hold water as an ant moat. In a garage or balcony bin, those four features are the difference between a system you maintain and one you fight.

The tradeoffs are equally structural. A fixed five-tray stack cannot grow with a bigger scrap stream — when you outgrow 50 liters, you replace the system rather than add a tray. The listing publishes no warranty, and the 2-pound weight figure is so implausible for a five-tray HDPE stack that we treat it as packaging data and rank the bin without it. The case for the Worm Factory 360

The WF360 is the design most worm-compost writing references, and its listing makes the classic argument cleanly: a nested four-tray stack where worms migrate upward toward fresh food, leaving finished castings in the lower trays to lift out whole. Four trays ship today, six are possible later — the system scales with your household instead of defining its ceiling on day one. The build is the stiffest in its price class at 11 pounds, the first tray ships with bedding, and the listing puts a 20-year limited warranty on parts and workmanship behind a made-in-the-USA product. For a buyer who wants the longest-lived bin at this price, nothing else on the board matches that warranty.

The costs are real too. As sold, it ships two fewer trays than the VermiHut at the same money. Its listing handles liquid and odor with claims rather than described mechanisms, and its product-details table carries an outright error — a capacity field reading 1 liter — which is why this comparison ranks it on tray count and build, not capacity. Who should buy which

  • Maximum capacity from day one → VermiHut Plus. Five trays and 50 liters the moment it arrives, plus the liquid, moisture, and ant engineering that decides whether a bin is pleasant to keep.
  • Growth path and warranty → Worm Factory 360. Four trays now, six later if the household’s scraps demand it, behind a 20-year limited warranty and the heaviest build on the board.
  • Tight floor space → VermiHut Plus. 17 inches square and comparatively low; the WF360 is close to 18 inches square and grows tall as trays stack.
  • A first bin for a household that might outgrow it → Worm Factory 360. The VermiHut is a finished system with a fixed ceiling; the WF360 is a chassis you extend.

FAQ

Which bin holds more?

As sold: VermiHut Plus — five trays from day one with 50 liters listed in the spec table. The Worm Factory 360 ships four trays and is listed as expandable to six, but its capacity field is a data-entry error (1 liter), so it gets no capacity credit in this comparison. If you actually stack it to six trays, the WF360 becomes the bigger system; the VermiHut never grows.

Which is easier to harvest?

Different mechanisms. The Worm Factory’s listed design is vertical migration: worms move up through stacked trays toward fresh food, leaving finished castings in the lowest tray to lift out. VermiHut’s listing markets the same continuous-flow idea plus a Worm Saver Tray that keeps worms out of the liquid collection area while you drain. Both designs work; neither is magic, and both assume you feed consistently.

Are VermiHut’s 50 liters and 2 pounds both real?

The 50 liters comes from the listing’s spec table and is used in this comparison. The 2-pound item weight does not survive a sniff test for a five-tray HDPE stack — it is treated here as a bin-only or packaging figure and is not used to rank the bin against the WF360’s 11 pounds.

Is the Worm Factory 360’s “1 liter” capacity real?

No. The Amazon product-details field reads 1 liter for a four-tray stacking system; it is a listing data-entry error. The honest move is to exclude it — this page ranks the WF360 on tray count and build instead, and flags the error rather than working around it.

Which one for an apartment?

VermiHut Plus, on footprint and odor engineering: 17 × 17 inches, a V-Board lid with coconut mat that the listing credits for moisture and odor control, and a one-piece liquid base. The Worm Factory 360 is roughly 18 inches square and gets nearly two feet tall when expanded, and its listing leans on a title-level “no odor” claim rather than a described mechanism. If your scrap stream will outgrow four trays, the WF360 is still the smarter long buy.
