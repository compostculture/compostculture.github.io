How long does composting actually take?

“Two weeks!” on a product box and “a year” from your neighbor are both true about different processes. Composting has two clocks — the hot, fast breakdown and the slow cure that decides whether the result is finished compost or just decomposing garbage. Here is what each method takes on each clock, from extensions data and well-run home systems.

Ranges assume a competently run system: right ratios, right moisture, season-appropriate starting material. The same tumbler fed only wet scraps with no browns will sit at “cold bin” speed with a smell problem attached. Article 04 compares the main methods head-to-head; the hot composting guide runs the fast method week by week.

The six dials that set your speed

  1. Size and insulation. Heat is insulation physics. Under roughly a cubic metre (~35 cu ft), a pile loses heat faster than microbes generate it, so the hot phase never starts — which is why small composters are, by definition, slow ones.
  2. Carbon-to-nitrogen balance. Microbes work fastest at 25:1–30:1. Starve them of nitrogen and they stall; give them too much and the excess leaves as ammonia while the pile goes sour.
  3. Moisture. 50–60% is the working band. A dry pile is a paused pile — the single most common reason a “hot” pile never heats in autumn, when everything going into it is dry leaves.
  4. Particle size. Decomposition happens at surfaces. Shredded material can work several times faster than the same material whole: a shredded corn stalk disappears in weeks; a whole one is still identifiable at next spring.
  5. Air. The thermophilic crew is aerobic. Turning when the center cools below ~120 °F re-oxygenates the core and restarts the hot run — each turn is a lap, not a chore. The right turning tool decides how many of those laps you actually make.
  6. Season. Cold slows everything. A hot pile built in November does its active work in February. Compost in the direction the calendar is already pushing: build the big hot batch in October when leaves are the whole newsstand.

How to tell it’s actually done

Forget calendars; test the material. Finished compost: sits at ambient temperature and stays there (a pile that reheats after turning is still cooking), breaks into crumbly pieces that show their own texture rather than the original item’s, smells earthy with no ammonia, sour, or swamp note, and has earthworms moving through the edges. The practical field test: fill a paper bag with a handful, seal it, and leave it a week. Open it — if it smells like the forest floor, it’s finished; if it smells like a landfill or has visibly reheated, it needs more cure.

Why “cool again” is not “done”

The cooling of a pile marks the end of the fast phase, not the end of the process. During curing the community restructures: fungi and slower microbes finish the stubborn material, and the mix stabilizes chemically. Skip the cure and you get compost that is genuinely useful for one thing — being left alone. Immature compost can still be phytotoxic: intermediate organic acids and released ammonia can damage germinating seeds and tender roots, which is why extension services consistently advise 1–3 months of curing before compost touches a vegetable bed.
