Composting in spring: the rebuild season — surge, restart, harvest.

Winter stores up; spring spends it. The thaw surge that finishes last year’s pile in weeks, the 40–50 °F restart trigger and what it means for each system — hot pile, tumbler, bin, worms, bokashi — the harvest hiding at the bottom of the pile, and the build checklist that turns a year of collected scraps into this season’s compost.

What the thaw actually does

Run the winter physics backward. Compost microbes climb the same ladder they slid down: reaction rates roughly 2–3× faster for every 10 °C (about 18 °F) of warming. Spring is worse for them than a simple warming — the pile thaws into a season’s worth of stored food at the same moment. Dormant microbes don’t ramp up; they rebound. The winter guide promises a surge that becomes visible within days, and that is what shows up in your pile the first warm week.

The moisture is already set for you, in both directions. Winter piles either dry out under covers or drown under melt, and both are common. The first spring job is the squeeze test — target the 50–60 % band used across every guide on this site — then re-wet a dry core or drain-and-blend a soaked one before you turn.

And the backlog is pre-chopped. Freeze–n—thaw cycles ruptured cell walls in leaves and scraps all winter, exactly the chopping a warm pile asks for by hand. The surge is biology spending your savings: a stockpile that looked inert in February disappears into a working hot pile within a spring month, if you feed it to something that can eat it.

Harvest what winter finished

Winter composts while you aren’t looking. Hot cores finish quietly under snow, slow bins finish from the bottom up, and early spring is the one moment the finished fraction sits there waiting — ambient, crumbly, nameable only as compost. Harvest it before you build the new season: a emptied bin is a bin you can feed all spring, and a pile base cleared of finished material gives the surge room to work.

Use the field test from the timeline guide: look, feel, smell — the material should be close to ambient temperature, break apart clean, and you shouldn’t be able to identify what it used to be. A layer that is still warm is not finished; it is working, and it goes back into the pile. The half-finished fraction isn’t a failure either — screened out and returned to the new build, it’s inoculum: last season’s microbes seeded into this season’s mix.

Timing matters twice over. Finished compost goes onto beds before planting and before the surge buries it under new material; the backlog of a year of scraps needs the same bins and piles you just cleared. Harvest first, then build — that order is most of the spring plan.

The new-season build checklist

  • Harvest first, then build. Empty the bins, clear the pile bases, screen what’s finished. The surge will fill every container you leave in place.
  • Inventory the winter backlog. Stockpile mound, bokashi buckets, freezer buckets, garage bin — write down which batch goes where: hot-pile edge, trench row, buried bokashi, worm-bin side feed.
  • Bank the spring browns. A surge without enough carbon goes anaerobic fast. Shredded leaves from the fall stockpile, cardboard, and straw go on hand for the 25–40:1 band the hot-pile guide runs on.
  • Rebuild or restart. If last fall’s core collapsed, build a new roughly-cubic-metre section of fresh mix now — warm weather is free labor the pile pays back in weeks.
  • Check the gear before the season, not mid-surge. Fork and aerator for turning cadence (tools page), a long-stem thermometer for the core (thermometer showdown), and reliable kitchen collection — the caddy roundup covers the counter end.
  • Restart the cadence, not just the pile. Turning schedule, feed schedule, and the bokashi burial plan all resume together; the winter stall gets paid off by rhythm, not one heroic weekend.
  • Read the pile, not the calendar. A thermometer reading and a squeeze test decide when to push — the first 50 °F week in a cold-climate yard is not the same as the last one.

Spring mistakes that cost you the season

  • Digesting the backlog in a sealed cold bin. A winter’s worth of stockpile dumped into a closed bin with no browns and no air is how spring announces itself — the rotten-egg thaw smell is anaerobic, and the recovery steps are in the fix-it guide. Feed the backlog to a hot pile’s edge or an open trench instead.
  • Spreading unfinished compost on new beds. Material still in its hot or cure phase ties up nitrogen mid-finish — exactly the wrong gift to seedlings. Harvest the finished fraction, return the rest, wait for the cure clock in the timeline guide.
  • Turning a soaked pile. Melt-saturated material mashes anaerobic when you turn it. Let it drain, blend in browns, then turn on a dry afternoon.
  • Overfeeding a cold worm bin. Outdoor colonies wake slowly; a spring feeding rate aimed at summer appetite rots the bin. Restart at roughly half rate and let the bin tell you when to climb (feed math in the worm guide).
  • Confusing the surge with finish. A pile heating fast is mid-process, not done — the cure is the second clock, and it’s where finish quality is decided.

The questions, answered

When do I restart my compost pile in spring?

When temperatures hold consistently above about 40–50 °F: re-wet the pile, work in fresh greens, restart the turning cadence, and expect the thaw surge to finish what winter stored up. The same trigger that ends the winter guide starts this one.

What is the spring thaw surge?

The rebound. Microbial rates climb roughly 2–3× for every 10 °C (18 °F) of warming — the winter slowdown run in reverse — and spring hands a thawed pile a season of stored, freeze-pre-chopped food at the same moment. Activity often becomes visible within days.

Can I harvest compost in early spring?

Yes — hot cores and slow bins finish quietly through winter. Harvest before you build the new season: finished material is close to room temperature, crumbly, and unrecognizable as its ingredients; anything half-finished goes back into the bin as inoculum.

When can I bury bokashi pre-compost?

As soon as the beds are workable. Bury the fermented mass at least two inches deep in a trench or hole, cover it, and it finishes in place in about two weeks. A winter of bokashi can be buried in one go.

What do I do with a winter of stockpiled scraps?

Feed them to something that can digest them fast: a hot pile’s edge, a spring trench layered with browns, or buried rows under a new build. Dumping a frozen backlog into a sealed cold bin is how spring gets its rotten-egg smell.
