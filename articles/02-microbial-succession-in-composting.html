Microbial Succession in Composting

The composting process is not a single event — it is a sequence of distinct phases, each dominated by different organisms and driven by different biochemical processes. Understanding this succession is the difference between knowing when to turn, aerate, or harvest, and doing it by guesswork.

October 2026 · 10 min read

The succession model

Think of composting like an ecological succession in a forest. When a field is cleared, pioneer species (fast-growing grasses and weeds) colonize first. As they die and decompose, conditions change to favor different species, eventually leading to a mature, stable community. Compost follows the same logic, but compressed into weeks rather than centuries.

Phase 1: The mesophilic stage (Days 0–3)

When organic materials are first assembled, the pile begins at ambient temperature. The first organisms to respond are mesophilic bacteria and yeasts, which metabolize the most readily available nutrients: simple sugars, starches, and amino acids.

This phase is characterized by:

  • Rapid microbial reproduction. Some bacterial species can double their population every 20–30 minutes under ideal conditions. The microbial biomass in a healthy pile increases by an order of magnitude within the first 48 hours.
  • Heat generation. Metabolic respiration releases heat. As the microbial population explodes, the pile temperature begins to rise.
  • Oxygen consumption. The rapid microbial activity depletes oxygen, which is why aeration is critical during this phase.

The mesophilic phase is short but decisive. If the C:N ratio is poorly balanced, or if oxygen becomes limiting, the process can stall here and the pile will turn anaerobic, producing foul odors instead of heat.

Phase 2: The thermophilic stage (Days 3–15)

As the temperature climbs past 40–45°C, mesophilic organisms are outcompeted or killed off. Thermophilic bacteria and fungi take over, and the pile enters the thermophilic phase, reaching temperatures of 55–70°C.

This is the most critical phase for two reasons:

  • Pathogen reduction. Sustained temperatures above 55°C for several days kill most pathogenic bacteria, weed seeds, and parasite eggs. This is the primary mechanism by which compost is "sanitized" without chemicals.
  • Accelerated decomposition. Thermophilic organisms produce enzymes that break down complex polymers (cellulose, hemicellulose, proteins) much faster than mesophiles can. The bulk of the visible decomposition happens during this phase.

The thermophilic phase is self-limiting. As the easily degradable materials are consumed, microbial activity slows and the temperature falls. In well-managed piles, this creates a "thermophilic spike" followed by a cooling curve — a pattern visible to anyone with a compost thermometer (our head-to-head of six models, from $15 to $60, is in Compost Thermometer Showdown).

Temperature is the most informative signal

Temperature tells you which phase you are in, which organisms are dominant, and whether pathogen kill is actually happening. Reading it well is cheap: a $15–$25 probe, used correctly, is the highest-value tool in a hot-composting kit.

Phase 3: The cooling stage (Days 10–40)

As the readily available substrates are consumed, the temperature drops back to ambient or slightly above. This phase is dominated by:

  • Fungi. Filamentous fungi (molds) specialize in breaking down lignin and other recalcitrant polymers that bacteria cannot easily digest. They penetrate materials with their mycelium, accessing carbon sources that are physically inaccessible to other organisms.
  • Actinomycetes. These bacteria-like organisms produce the characteristic "earthy" smell of compost. They break down tough materials like leaf litter, straw, and bark, and contribute significantly to the formation of humus-like compounds.
  • Protozoa and nematodes. These microscopic animals graze on bacteria and fungal spores, releasing excess nitrogen in plant-available forms (ammonium, NH4+). Their presence is an indicator of a mature, balanced compost.

This phase is slower than the thermophilic stage but is critical for the quality of the final product. The breakdown of lignin and the formation of stable humus compounds happen here, not in the hot phase.

Phase 4: The curing stage (Days 30–90+)

Curing is the final maturation phase, where the compost stabilizes and the microbial community reaches a relatively stable equilibrium. The temperature is now at ambient, and the material has become dark, crumbly, and indistinguishable from its original components.

During curing:

  • Humification. The partially decomposed organic matter undergoes chemical reactions that form humus — stable, complex compounds that resist further decomposition and improve soil structure.
  • Nutrient mineralization. Organic nitrogen is converted to plant-available ammonium and nitrate, making nutrients accessible to plants.
  • Community stabilization. The microbial community reaches a dynamic equilibrium, with diverse populations of bacteria, fungi, and animals coexisting in a stable food web.

The length of the curing phase depends on the initial C:N ratio, the types of materials used, and environmental conditions. A well-balanced pile can cure in 3–4 weeks; a high-lignin pile (bark, straw) may take 2–3 months.

Why the thermophilic phase matters most

The thermophilic phase is the bottleneck for most backyard composters. If a pile fails to reach thermophilic temperatures, it stalls in the mesophilic phase and produces a product that is slow to mature and may harbor pathogens. The factors that determine whether a pile reaches thermophilic conditions:

What drives a pile thermophilic — and what keeps it cool
ConditionFavorableUnfavorable
Pile size> 1 m³ (insulation)< 0.5 m³ (heat loss)
Moisture50–60%< 30% (dry) or > 70% (anaerobic)
OxygenRegular turning (every 3–5 days)Matting, compaction
Carbon sourceSufficient easily degradable carbonToo much lignin (bark), too little nitrogen

The practical implication: a small pile of mostly bark chips will never thermophilize, regardless of how often you turn it. You need enough insulating mass, enough moisture, and enough readily available carbon to generate heat faster than it radiates away.

The management framework

Putting it all together, here is the decision framework for managing a compost pile based on its phase:

  • If the pile is cold (< 40°C) and has been sitting for more than 3 days: it is stalled in the mesophilic phase. Add nitrogen (greens, manure) and moisture, then turn. If it still does not heat up, the pile is too small or too carbon-rich — add volume and more nitrogen.
  • If the pile is hot (55–70°C): you are in the thermophilic phase. Do not turn it more than once every 3–5 days — over-aeration cools it down. Let the thermophiles do their work. This is also the phase where pathogen kill happens, so if you are composting manure or pet waste, aim for sustained temperatures above 55°C for at least 3 days.
  • If the pile has cooled below 40–45°C: you are in the cooling or curing phase. Turn it once to introduce fungi and aerate, then check back in 7–10 days. If the material is still recognizable, it is not ready to use as a soil amendment — let it cure longer.
  • If the material is dark, crumbly, and smells like forest floor: it is done. Sieve it, use the fines as compost, and put the coarse material back in a new pile to finish decomposing.

Bottom line

Succession is a relay: fast mesophiles start the fire, thermophiles carry it to the pathogen-kill range, fungi and actinomycetes finish the tough material, and a stable community matures the humus. Each handoff is triggered by what the previous team ate — and each one has an observable temperature, odor, and texture signature. Know the signature of the phase you are in, and the management action follows from it rather than from habit.
