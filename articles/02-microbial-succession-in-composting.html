Microbial Succession in Composting

October 2026 · 10 min read

Compost is not a static product but a living process. The most important concept in understanding compost culture is microbial succession — the predictable, sequential replacement of one microbial community by another as environmental conditions change.

The Succession Model Explained

Microbial succession in compost follows a pattern remarkably similar to ecological succession in forests or coral reefs. As organisms modify their environment (through metabolism, heat production, pH changes), they create conditions that favor different species while making the environment unsuitable for those that came before.

This is not random. Each phase of decomposition creates specific chemical and physical conditions — temperature, moisture, pH, oxygen availability, substrate type — that act as environmental filters selecting for particular microbial groups.

Phase 1: Mesophilic Colonization (Days 0–3)

When fresh organic material is assembled into a compost pile, the first colonizers are mesophilic bacteria and fungi — organisms that thrive at moderate temperatures (20–40°C). These include:

  • Bacillus species — rapid growers that consume simple sugars and proteins
  • Pseudomonas species — versatile decomposers of carbohydrates
  • Clostridium species — anaerobic fermenters active in the pile's interior
  • Saccharomyces (yeast) — consumers of simple sugars and alcohols

The mesophilic phase is characterized by rapid microbial growth, heat production from metabolic activity, and a gradual decline in available simple substrates. The pile temperature rises as bacterial metabolism generates heat faster than it can dissipate.

Phase 2: Thermophilic Transition (Days 3–15)

As the pile reaches temperatures above 40°C, mesophilic organisms begin to decline while thermophiles — heat-loving bacteria and actinomycetes — take over. Key players include:

  • Bacillus stearothermophilus — a classic thermophile active at 50–65°C
  • Solibacter thermophilus — cellulose-degrading thermophile
  • Thermoactinomyces species — actinomycetes that produce geosmin (the earthy smell)
  • Methanobacterium — methanogens active in anaerobic microsites

The thermophilic phase is the most critical for pathogen reduction and weed seed destruction. Temperatures above 55°C sustained for several days kill most harmful organisms while preserving beneficial ones.

Phase 3: Cooling and Fungal Colonization (Days 10–40)

As simpler substrates are consumed, the pile cools. This cooling allows fungi to re-establish dominance. Filamentous fungi like Trichoderma, Aspergillus, and Penicillium break down complex polymers — cellulose, hemicellulose, lignin — that bacteria cannot easily digest.

This phase is characterized by:

  • Fungal hyphae penetrating deeper into undecomposed material
  • Production of extracellular enzymes (cellulases, laccases, peroxidases)
  • Gradual stabilization of pH toward neutral
  • Increase in protozoan and nematode populations as bacterial food sources decline

Phase 4: Curing and Stabilization (Days 30–90+)

The final phase is characterized by a diverse, stable microbial community that slowly mineralizes remaining organic matter into humus. This phase can extend for months and is critical for producing mature compost.

Why Succession Matters for Compost Management

Understanding succession allows us to manage compost more effectively:

  • Aeration timing: Turning the pile during the thermophilic phase (not too early, not too late) maximizes pathogen reduction while preserving beneficial organisms.
  • Substrate selection: Adding lignin-rich materials (bark, straw) extends the fungal colonization phase and produces more stable humus.
  • Moisture management: Maintaining 40–60% moisture supports bacterial activity in early phases while preventing anaerobic conditions that favor pathogens.
  • C:N ratio adjustment: Balancing nitrogen-rich (greens) and carbon-rich (browns) materials ensures the pile doesn't stall at any successional stage.

Practical Implications

The succession model explains why compost takes time. Rushing the process by turning too frequently disrupts fungal networks and resets succession to earlier bacterial-dominated phases. Conversely, leaving a pile unturned for months allows it to stabilize naturally through the full successional sequence.

Inoculation Techniques

One of the most powerful tools in compost management is inoculation — introducing specific microbial cultures at key stages to accelerate or direct succession. There are several approaches:

  • Mature compost tea: A liquid extract from finished compost contains a diverse community of bacteria, fungi, protozoa, and nematodes. Spraying this on fresh feedstock jump-starts the mesophilic phase.
  • Bokashi bran: Fermented bran inoculated with effective microorganisms (EM) provides a concentrated source of lactic acid bacteria and yeast that rapidly acidify the pile, suppressing pathogens while encouraging beneficial fermentation.
  • Mycorrhizal spore suspensions: Adding mycorrhizal fungi at the curing stage accelerates humus formation by establishing symbiotic relationships with plant roots in the finished compost.
  • Thermophilic culture starters: Commercial products containing Bacillus stearothermophilus and Thermoactinomyces species can be added to ensure rapid thermophilic development, particularly useful in cold climates or winter composting.

The choice of inoculation strategy depends on the desired outcome. For fast decomposition (e.g., kitchen waste processing), a mesophilic starter accelerates the initial phase. For disease suppression, a thermophilic culture ensures pathogen-killing temperatures are reached quickly. For soil amendment quality, a fungal-focused inoculant at the curing stage produces more stable humus.

The Succession Model as a Management Framework

The succession model is more than academic description — it provides a practical framework for decision-making:

  • When to turn: Turn during the thermophilic peak (days 5–10) for maximum pathogen reduction. Avoid turning too early (disrupts bacterial colonization) or too late (fungal networks already established).
  • When to harvest: Harvest when temperature has stabilized at ambient levels and the material is uniformly dark brown — this signals completion of the curing phase.
  • When to add new feedstock: Add fresh materials during the cooling phase (days 10–20) so they are incorporated into the active thermophilic zone rather than sitting on top as undecomposed waste.
