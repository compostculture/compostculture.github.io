Compost tea: what the evidence says.

Compost tea gets both the most hype and the most dismissal of anything in composting — usually from people who never brewed it. Here’s the middle ground: what an aerated compost extract reliably does, what it doesn’t, and why the aerator is the entire difference between tea and a bucket of swamp water.

What compost tea actually is

A compost tea is water that has been steeped with compost — for home brewing, usually vermicompost — while a continuous airstone keeps the liquid saturated with oxygen. The aeration is not a convenience feature; it is the definition. Aerobic conditions during the brew encourage the same bacterial communities that live in healthy soil, and the finished liquid is a suspension of those organisms plus the soluble compounds the compost released. That is the whole product: a living inoculant, not a fertilizer.

The reason the distinction matters: without aeration, the same steep goes anaerobic within hours, and anaerobic broths select for a different and less desirable microbial cast — the stuff that smells like a stagnant pond. The two liquids are visually similar and functionally opposite.

What the evidence says — without the hype

The honest summary of the research literature is “mixed, with a few consistent threads.”

  • Soil biology: the most replicated finding is that tea applications shift soil microbial communities toward the compost’s profile and can raise microbial activity. That is real, and it is the strongest case for brewing at all.
  • Disease suppression: several trials report reduced incidence of certain foliar and soil-borne diseases after regular applications. The effect is strain- and crop-specific, so treat it as a possible benefit, not a guaranteed fungicide.
  • Yield and growth: results here are the noisiest. Some studies show modest benefits, many show none, and a fair number show nothing beyond what the same compost applied as solid mulch would do. If you are buying a pump expecting a yield lift, the literature does not support that expectation.

The practical translation: compost tea is a reasonable, low-cost way to spread the biology from a good compost through your garden — and it is not a fertilizer, a fungicide, or a replacement for balanced nutrition. If your soil is nutrient-deficient, no brew fixes that; if your soil has no life in it, the brew is one of the few things that can help.

The one rule: air, without a gap

The single failure mode of home tea brewing is the pump dying mid-brew. An airstone that stops for an hour or two has, in effect, committed the brew to the anaerobic side — the organisms that dominated the aerobic phase start to die off, and the broth turns. The rules that follow from this:

  • Brew where the power is reliable; a cheap aquarium pump is fine, but a pump that dies silently is not.
  • Keep the brew at room temperature — heat accelerates the anaerobic shift once aeration stops.
  • Stop at 24–48 hours and use it fast. The finished tea is a living liquid with a short shelf life; the common window is within four hours of stopping the aeration, and sooner in warm weather.

The 5-gallon recipe

  1. Dechlorinate the water. Five gallons of tap water, off-gassed 24 hours in an open bucket, or rainwater. Chlorine kills the very inoculant you are trying to grow. If you have a source of vermicompost from your own bin, that is your inoculant; otherwise a bag of quality vermicompost works. If you brew from manure-based compost, keep it off the leaves of anything you eat raw.
  2. Load the inoculant. One to two cups of vermicompost into a mesh bag, knotted and suspended in the bucket. The bag keeps the solids out of the pour without any racking step.
  3. Add the food source. One ounce of unsulfured molasses is the common addition — a fast carbon source that feeds the bacteria through the brew. Optional, but it makes a more vigorous, darker brew. Stir everything in.
  4. Run the air. Airstone in, pump on, lid on (the air goes through the bag, not over the top). The surface should be a steady, fine fizz for the whole brew. Leave it at room temperature, out of direct sun.
  5. Stop and use. After 24–48 hours the liquid should be dark amber and smell earthy — like soil after rain, not sour. Stop the pump, remove the bag, and use the tea within four hours. A sour smell at the stop point means the brew went anaerobic somewhere; dump it and check the pump before the next one.

Applying it right

Two uses, both straightforward:

  • Foliar spray. Fine mist on the leaf surfaces, early in the morning or late in the evening so the film dries slowly and the organisms have a chance to settle. A trigger bottle (like the photo up top) is the standard tool; a backpack sprayer for a whole garden.
  • Soil drench. Pour or hose the tea into the soil around the root zone. This is the more forgiving application and the one to start with.

Cadence: a drench every few weeks through the growing season is a reasonable schedule; more frequent is not better, and the biology in the soil does the work between applications, not the calendar. If you only ever have time for one application a season, a spring soil drench is the one with the most to show for it.

The anaerobic version: compost steep

If you do not want to buy a pump, the honest alternative is a compost steep: compost left in water for a day or two without aeration. It is a weaker, less predictable liquid, and the anaerobic conditions mean a real (if small) pathogen consideration if the compost contains manure — which is why a vermicompost or plant-based steep, applied to soil rather than leaves, is the sensible version. It is a legitimate, cheaper liquid for a soil drench, but it is a different product than tea, and it should not be sold as tea. The drain-pan liquid from a worm bin is a third, even simpler cousin — see the vermicomposting guide for the dilution ratio.
