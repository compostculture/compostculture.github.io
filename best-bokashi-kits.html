Best Bokashi Kits: 5 Compared

Bokashi is a different animal from the hot pile you turn outside: it's an anaerobic fermentation. In a sealed, airless bucket, effective microorganisms (EM) and a carbon carrier (bran) break down raw food scraps — meat, dairy, the stuff a hot pile will never take — in a week or two, with no heat, no turning, and no smell. The kit differences that actually matter are capacity, what's in the box, and whether the two-bin rotation matches your rhythm. Here are the five setups that cover every kitchen, chosen by how much waste you really throw away.

Updated October 2026 · Two brands, one DIY path

How we pick a bokashi kit

Four questions, all about your kitchen rather than the brand:

  • How much kitchen waste you generate. A single 4–5 gallon bucket takes roughly one to two weeks of scraps for a small household. If you're cooking for three or four people every day, you'll outpace a single bucket mid-rotation — that's the whole point of the two-bin kits.
  • One bucket or two. The method is a two-stage flow: fill bucket A, let it ferment a week or two, bury or pot that batch, then start on bucket B. A single bucket means a pause between batches (you wait to bury batch one before refilling). A two-bin kit means you're always fermenting and never stopped.
  • What's actually in the box. A real "starter kit" should ship the bucket, the EM culture, and the bran — the carbon and the food for the microbes. The cheapest "bucket + bran" options assume you already have the EM or use a powder; it's cheaper, but one more thing to buy and measure.
  • The all-season point. Bokashi ferments at room temperature, so it keeps working in the dead of winter while an outdoor hot pile goes dormant. This is the one composting method that doesn't shut down in January.

Worth knowing: the liquid is a byproduct, not a failure

Every bokashi bucket drips a thin liquid as it ferments. People new to the method take that for a leak or a problem; it's actually one of the two outputs. Drain it off, dilute it (a common ratio is about 1:100 with water), and it works as a liquid feed for plants. The solid fermented mass is the other output — it's not finished compost, it's a pre-compost that turns into finished compost in roughly two weeks once you bury it. That's the pairing to make: a long-handled fork to dig the trench to bury the mass in. The rules that beat the hardware

  • Bokashi is anaerobic — keep the lid tight. The whole method depends on no oxygen getting in. A lid that seals loosely lets air in, and what you have stops being "fermenting" and starts being "rotting" (smell, surface mold). Press the latch down on every addition.
  • Press scraps down and top with a thin layer of bran. Compact the waste and dust a little bran over it with each add. The bran is both the carbon and the food for the EM — skip it and the culture starves.
  • Bury the finished mass; don't just pile it on top. The fermented batch isn't done — it's pre-compost. Dig a trench at least a few inches deep, drop it in, and cover. In about two weeks it breaks down into usable compost in place.
  • Use the liquid or lose it. If you let it sit in the base, it's just a mess to clean. Drain it, dilute it, and use it as a feed — or pour a little down the drain if you'd rather not deal with it at all.
  • This is your winter method. When the outdoor hot pile is frozen and the worms are slowed, the bokashi bucket keeps running at room temperature. It's the reason most serious composters keep one in the kitchen year-round.
