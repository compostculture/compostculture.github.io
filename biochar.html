Biochar: 5 Bags Compared (and What It Actually Does in Your Pile)

Biochar is charcoal made for the soil, not the fire: pyrolyzed in low oxygen at controlled temperature so the pore structure survives. In a compost system it plays two jobs — a water-holding sponge and protected real estate for the microbial community. The catch most reviews skip is that raw biochar is hydrophobic and temporarily hungry for nutrients, so how it's activated decides whether it helps or hobbles your pile. Here are the five bags you can actually buy, by material, activation state, and coverage.

Updated October 2026 · Five bags, three brands

What biochar does — and what it doesn't

  • In the pile: a sponge and a habitat. The pores hold water against drought and give microbes a surface that survives the pH swings a pile goes through. It's an aid to the process, not a fuel — biochar is inert carbon, so it adds no nitrogen and no heat.
  • In the soil: the longer game. The research that's most consistent is on soil application — water retention in dry conditions and a stable carbon reservoir that, unlike ordinary compost, isn't re-mineralized in a season. That's the whole terra preta idea in one line.
  • The activation problem (read this before buying raw). Freshly made biochar repels water, and its bare pores can temporarily absorb nutrients — including nitrogen — out of the soil around it. Two honest fixes: mix raw char into active or finished compost for a few weeks to charge it, or buy a product that's pre-inoculated or blended with humic acid, which does the activation for you.
  • Where to put it. The best use in a home system is as a finisher's additive: folded into the last stage of a batch, mixed into worm-bin bedding, or worked into soil where finished compost is going. Dumping a bag of raw char into a fresh pile is the version that disappoints.

Use the char right

  • Raw means activate first. Mix into active or finished compost for a few weeks, or soak in compost tea. Buying pre-inoculated or humic-blended (FOOP, HumiChar) skips this step.
  • Wet the raw before it meets soil. Dry raw char is hydrophobic — pre-wetting is the minimum if you're charging it yourself.
  • Mix, don't mound. A handful to a cup or two per 10 gallons of compost, or the coverage rate on the bag for soil. Concentrated pockets are what draw the temporary nutrient-hunger.
  • It's carbon, not food. Biochar doesn't feed plants. The humic acid in HumiChar adds some of that chemistry; the char itself is the house the microbes live in.
  • It stays. Unlike compost, which respires its carbon back out within a season, biochar is stable for decades in soil. That's the reason to use it, and why one application is a long-term decision — do it where you'll actually keep gardening.
