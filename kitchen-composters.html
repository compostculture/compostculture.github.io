Countertop Electric Composters: 6 Machines Compared

A countertop electric composter shrinks a drum of wet waste right on the kitchen counter — the dehydrators do it with heat and airflow in one to four hours, and the microbial machines (the Reencle Prime and both GEME bio-reactors) let a living colony digest it instead. What it gives you is the removal of the fridge bin, the summer-smelling pail, and the biweekly trip to the outdoor pile — and a daily thirty-second habit. What it does not give you is garden-ready compost by week one: the output is pre-compost (the GEME bio-reactors are the exception — they produce an active compost base), and the last step (burying it in soil or feeding it to a pile) is still on you. Here are the six machines we'd consider, by drum size, daily throughput, and the ongoing consumable cost.

Updated October 2026 · Six machines, 3 L to 19 L

What the machine actually does (so the marketing lands)

  • Dehydration or microbial digestion — the drum decides. The dehydrators run at high temperature with airflow (vacuum in some designs) and reduce the moisture in your scraps by roughly an order of magnitude; the Reencle Prime and the two GEME bio-reactors skip the heat and let a microbial colony digest the material instead. Either way the output is a pre-compost — what Lomi itself labels "pre-compost" for the dehydrators, and an active substrate for the bio-reactors — that finishes in soil in a few weeks at two inches or more of cover, or in a live pile.
  • The real product is convenience. The machine's job is to take the waste stream out of your kitchen's decision tree: no bag in the fridge, no sealed container that smells in July, no hauling wet scraps outdoors. If you're not buying it for that, you're buying the wrong category.
  • Throughput is the spec that matters. The 3–4-liter class handles on the order of a pound or two of wet waste per cycle. A realistic ceiling for a one- or two-person household is about two pounds a day; the 14–19-liter class raises that (Reencle rates 2.2 lb/day; GEME claims up to 11 lb/day, which is the marketing ceiling, not a measured number). If your household generates more than ~3 lb of scraps daily, a countertop machine alone won't keep up.
  • Consumables split this category in two. Lomi's cycle chemistry requires Lomi Pods (plus a replaceable carbon filter) — a real per-cycle cost after the initial bundle runs out. The Growell, Ouaken, and GEME machines in this comparison need nothing but a rinse; the Reencle's only recurring part is a long-cycle carbon filter (maker-quoted life of roughly 9–12 months). Factor that into year-2 cost before you buy.
  • Energy is not the argument. A 300–500 W machine running one to four hours per cycle, even twice a day, costs a dollar or two a day at typical US rates. The case for these machines is convenience, odor control, and landfill diversion — not electricity savings.

The batch drum that pays back its sticker price

The Reencle Prime is the drum that pays back its sticker price. It looks like a countertop dehydrator and it works in batches like one — but inside, nothing is baked. A colony of microorganisms digests each load over a cycle of 2.5 to 24 hours, in five operating modes, and the purification that keeps it quiet is a 3-layer carbon filter on a roughly 12-month replacement cycle, not a per-cycle pod. It's the biggest drum in the batch class at the lowest price on the page — 14 L at $499, versus 3–4 L drums at $299–399 — and the only batch drum that takes meat and dairy; the maker's own copy even claims bones.

The Amazon listing's headline number is 2.2 lb of food waste per day — a ceiling, not a floor. Check it against your own scrap weight before you buy: a household pushing more than that, or a very meat-heavy mix, will feel the limit. What you empty every 1–3 months is dry, crumbly pre-compost with roughly 90% of the volume gone (manufacturer claim) — it still wants a final stretch of maturation in soil, a bed, or a worm bin.

Run the machine right

  • Know the exclusions. No pet waste, for any of them. Meat and dairy split the lineup: the GEME bio-reactors are marketed to take meat, dairy, and small bones, and the Reencle Prime's maker lists meat, dairy, and even bones among its accepted inputs. The Lomi publishes its exclusions (meat, dairy, pizza crusts); the Growell and Ouaken publish per-model lists. Check yours before you assume. Fruit and vegetable scraps, coffee grounds, and bread are the reliable inputs.
  • Chop to drum scale. The dehydration happens across the surface of the scraps. Big chunks with wet centers are what leave you digging into the output with your fingers.
  • Rinse after every cycle. A quick water rinse keeps the drum from curing on. Machines with a self-clean cycle (the 4-liter class) want one run a week on top of that.
  • The output is stage one. Bury it at least two inches deep in soil and give it a few weeks, or fold it into a live pile. It's pre-compost — the machine did the dehydration, not the decomposition.
  • Do the throughput math before you buy. Estimate your household's daily scrap weight, then check it against the machine's per-cycle capacity and the cycles you'll actually run. If the math doesn't close, the machine becomes a second fridge bin for pre-dried scraps.

The questions, answered

Do countertop composters make compost?

Not finished compost — pre-compost. Every machine here reduces the moisture and volume of your scraps and leaves a substrate that still needs soil or a live pile to finish. The dehydrators (Lomi, Growell, Ouaken) dry the material into a storable flake with most of its original structure intact; the microbial machines (Reencle Prime, GEME Terra 2, GEME Pro) digest it further with a living colony. The last stage — a few weeks of microbial work in soil — happens outside every one of these machines.

Can I put meat and dairy in a countertop composter?

Only in the three microbial machines. The GEME Terra 2 and Pro are marketed to take meat, dairy, and small bones, and the Reencle Prime's maker lists meat, dairy, and even bones among its accepted inputs. The dehydrators work by drying, and their published input lists stop at produce, vegetable scraps, and grounds like coffee and tea. No machine on the page takes pet waste.

What do these machines cost after the purchase?

The Lomi is the one with a per-cycle consumable — pods, budgeted against the $399 bundle. The Reencle Prime's only recurring part is a carbon filter with a maker-quoted life of roughly 9–12 months. The Growell, Ouaken, and both GEMEs publish no per-cycle consumable at all. No machine on the page has a subscription; the GEME links are partner links, the rest are Amazon affiliate.

How much waste can one of these handle?

The Growell and Ouaken (4 L drums) are sized to a one-person feed rate. The Lomi 1 takes 550–1,100 g per batch. The Reencle Prime claims up to 2.2 lb (1 kg) a day for a two-person household. The GEMEs are the high-throughput tier: a 14 L chamber (Terra 2, 2 kg/day claim) and a 19 L chamber (Pro, 5 kg/day claim for a family of five).

Do I run a cycle, or just feed it?

The dehydrators and the Reencle Prime are batch machines: load the drum, start a run, and empty the residue when the cycle ends (2–24 hours for the Reencle). The GEME bio-reactors are continuous: feed the hopper and the chamber digests on its own cycle, emptying roughly monthly for a household.
