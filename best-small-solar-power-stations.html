How we picked in this class

Under 500 Wh the marketing noise gets louder as the prices get smaller, so the bar is the one this site uses everywhere, applied to the listings:

  • Output ceiling before capacity. A station can only run tools under its AC rating — the pick order here follows who can actually run the loads composting generates, not who stores the most watt-hours.
  • Surge headroom, with a planning number. Motor tools spike at 2–3× nameplate on startup; a station without a published surge rating (two of the batteries below) gets flagged instead of assumed.
  • A rating base worth trusting. Three candidates carry 1,355 to 11,424 Amazon ratings as their verification base; one shows 73, and it is labeled as unproven rather than quietly included.
  • Storefront honesty. Two of the six buy best direct from the brand store — UDPOWER's own site carries the C-series prices — so the links go direct. Where a number wasn't displayed on a listing we verified, the table prints an em dash instead of a guess.
  • Prices are snapshots, not promises. This class swings hard on promo cycles; the observed strikethrough spreads are printed with the prices.

What this class can actually run

Two numbers decide everything here, and capacity isn't first. AC output is the gate: a 200 W station will not run a 600 W tool no matter how full the battery is. Capacity is the clock: once a tool fits under the output ceiling, runtime is rated Wh × 0.85 ÷ load watts — the 0.85 stands in for inverter and cold losses, so treat every figure below as a planning estimate and round down. Motor tools add one more check: startup surge runs roughly 2–3× nameplate, and that spike has to fit under the station's surge rating.

  • Compost tea pump (aquarium class, 3–8 W typical). Elite 30 V2: 288 × 0.85 ÷ 5 ≈ 49 hours — it covers the tea guide's 48-hour max brew with margin. C200: 192 × 0.85 ÷ 5 ≈ 33 hours — overnight done, top up the next day.
  • Work light (~10 W). Every battery on this page runs 15+ hours; the C200 about 16.
  • Small powered cultivator or aerator (300–600 W nameplate). The Elite 30 V2 and Explorer 300 both run a 300 W tool for about 50 minutes (288 and 292 Wh × 0.85 ÷ 300). The Elite keeps going where the Jackery's 300 W ceiling stops it — a 600 W nameplate gets roughly 25 minutes on BLUETTI and nothing at all on the Jackery, the C200, or the 240 v2. The C400 takes 300 W-class tools for about 45 minutes.
  • Phone, tablet, laptop (5–60 W). Trivial hours on every unit; a 256 Wh pack runs a 50 W laptop for four-plus hours.

Solar recharge in the field

Every station here takes solar, but the useful panel size is capped by the station's solar input ceiling — check that line on the spec sheet before buying any panel. The arithmetic to plan with: usable harvest per day ≈ panel watts × 0.6 × 5–6 good-sun hours. The verified C400 + 40 W bundle therefore harvests roughly 120–145 Wh on a clear day — most of the pack's 256 Wh, but not a guaranteed full refill; the 100 W Renogy kit doubles that math, which is exactly why it earned pick 06. The deeper physics — input ceilings, series voltage, why bigger arrays stop helping — is the solar section of the showdown page.

FAQ

Why does a composting site cover small batteries?

Because the compost work happens where the outlets aren’t: brewing tea at the pile, lighting an evening turn, running a small cultivator in a corner of the plot. The site already covers the 1 kWh+ tier on the showdown page — this is the carry-to-the-pile tier, where the right answer is usually $130–$290 instead of $500+.

What do you actually need to run at the pile?

Count the loads first. An aquarium-class tea pump draws single-digit watts — any unit here handles days of it. A work light is the same story. A powered cultivator or aerator is the fork: 300–600 W nameplate means you need 300 W+ output and published surge headroom, and runtime measured in tens of minutes, not hours. Read your tool’s nameplate before you read any station’s marketing.

Can a sub-300 Wh station run a compost tea brew?

Yes. At a typical 5 W pump the 288 Wh Elite 30 V2 covers the tea guide’s 48-hour maximum brew (about 49 estimated hours); the 192 Wh C200 gets you about 33 — finish with a daytime top-up or start on the bigger pack. The guide’s hard limit still applies: tea goes static at 48 hours regardless of how much battery you bring.

Capacity or output — which decides the pick?

Output decides what runs at all; capacity decides how long it runs. In this class the 200 W / 300 W / 600 W output tiers are the real shelf divisions — most “smallest and cheapest” searches end at the C200, most “will it run my tool” searches end at the Elite 30 V2.

Do small stations ship with solar panels?

Usually not. The one verified exception on this page is UDPOWER’s C400 plus 40 W panel bundle at $239.99. Every other pairing is a separate purchase — which is why the Renogy 100 W kit is on the board: a station plus a panel bought together is a system, and a station bought alone is just a battery you refill at the wall.
