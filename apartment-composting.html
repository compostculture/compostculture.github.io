Composting in an apartment.

No yard, close neighbors, and a kitchen the size of a closet do not rule out composting — they rule out exactly one thing: the open pile. Every serious indoor system works by containment: a colony of worms, a sealed ferment, a machine, or a freezer and a drop-off. Here is each one with its real numbers and its real obligations.

The constraints, stated honestly

  • Balconies are not yards. An open bin or tumbler on a balcony is wind-blown material, fruit-fly highway, and a neighbor complaint waiting to happen. Treat the balcony as storage at best, not as the digester.
  • Odor is a technique failure, not a method feature. A balanced worm bin under a sink smells like soil. A sour, ammonia, or swamp smell always means the same few things: too much food, too few browns, or no air. Those are fixable, and the fix guide keys each smell to its cause.
  • Finished compost needs somewhere to go. An apartment produces compost faster than an apartment consumes it. Houseplants and a few balcony pots absorb the first gallon; after that you need a plan — a community garden that takes it, a friend with a yard, or a building's green-waste program that accepts finished material, not just fresh scraps.
  • Pests follow food left visible. Every indoor system that stays pest-free does it by containment: lids, buried food, sealed ferments. The systems below fail in exactly one direction — toward openness.

The four systems that work indoors, ranked

  1. Worm bin — the default answer. A tray-style bin (the common 4-tray format runs about 18×18″ footprint and 28″ tall) under a sink or in a closet handles a solo kitchen comfortably: red wigglers eat 25–50% of their body weight a day, so 2–4 lb of worms covers 0.5–1 lb of scraps daily. What it will not take: meat, dairy, oily food, citrus in quantity. What it gives back: castings, the best potting amendment an apartment can manufacture — mix 10–20% into houseplant soil. The full setup with feed-rate math is the vermicomposting guide; the bins worth buying are compared in best worm bins.
  2. Bokashi — the one that eats what worms refuse. Two 5-gallon buckets under rotation: fill one, sprinkle inoculated bran, press the air out, cap it; ~2 weeks later it ferments into a pickled-smelling pre-compost. Meat, dairy, cooked food — all in. The catch is real: fermented material must be buried to finish, in a shared yard, a community plot, or a big bin. Bokashi shines when paired with a drop-off route; alone it moves the problem downstream. The mechanics are on the bokashi page, the kits worth buying in best bokashi kits.
  3. Countertop machines — convenience with an asterisk. Electric composters split into two camps: dehydrator-style units that grind and dry scraps into a sterile-looking, non-compost topping, and bio-reactor units that keep the material biologically active. Neither is finished compost; both reduce kitchen volume and odor immediately. The honest cost math — power, consumables, and 3-year totals — is in the countertop showdown, with full reviews of the GEME Terra 2, the GEME Pro 19L, and the Reencle Prime 14L.
  4. Freezer + drop-off — zero-infrastructure option. Scraps go into a bag in the freezer (smell and fruit flies cannot exist in a freezer) and travel weekly to a community garden, farmers-market pickup, or municipal green-waste program if your city runs one. No biology on your side of the deal at all — the cheapest working “system” in an apartment, limited only by whether a drop-off exists near you.

Smell and pests: the prevention list

Indoor composting fails in three ways and all three are preventable by habit. Overfeeding an undersized worm bin — food sits, ferments, sours; freeze scraps between feedings and the problem mostly disappears. Missing browns — every handful of scraps wants a handful of shredded cardboard on top; it is the single highest-leverage habit in indoor compost. Wrong material in the wrong system — meat and dairy go in bokashi or a machine, never in the worm bin. The full material rules by system are in what can and can’t go in a compost bin.
