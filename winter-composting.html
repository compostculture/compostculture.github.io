Composting in winter: what actually slows down — and what doesn’t.

Cold weather doesn’t stop composting; it changes which systems win. The temperature physics behind a sluggish January pile, a seven-method playbook for the season — hot pile, tumbler, bin, worms, bokashi — and the spring restart that pays for everything you bank between now and March.

What actually changes when it gets cold

Cold does not kill a compost pile — it demotes it. Compost microbes run on enzyme chemistry, and enzyme speed follows the rule every introductory biochemistry text uses: reaction rates fall roughly 2–3× for every 10 °C (about 18 °F) of cooling. A pile that hit 140 °F in October is not dead in January; its thermophiles have handed off to slow mesophiles that creep toward finished. The process never switches off — it drops a gear.

What decides who stays hot is geometry. A self-heating pile of properly mixed material at roughly a cubic metre (about 35 cu ft) or more produces heat faster than it sheds it, and can keep a hot core right through winter under snow. A 37-gallon tumbler or a small bin has high surface area, no insulation, and goes cold in hard weather — same biology, different physics. That single difference decides your whole winter plan.

And frozen material is not ruined material. Freezing puts microbes dormant, not extinct, and freeze–n—thaw cycles genuinely help: ice ruptures cell walls in leaves and scraps, pre-chopping them for spring. The cold season is a slow season, not a dead one.

Keep feeding: three winter scrap plans

Plan one: the stockpile. Keep collecting exactly as you do all year — sealed kitchen caddy, emptied every few days — and dump each week’s scraps into a dedicated mound of browns: a bin of shredded leaves, cardboard, or a tarp pile of carbon. The scraps sit cold and fly-free until the ground (or a hot pile) is ready for them in spring. If you already freeze scraps between collections, the freezer bucket is the same plan in miniature: compost doesn’t care how material spent its cold months.

Plan two: bokashi. Fermentation under the sink is a season-proof process — the bokashi bran and its microbes work at room temperature while it’s 20 °F outside. The finished pre-compost keeps well bagged or bucketed for weeks, so a winter of bokashi can be buried in one go when beds open up. The bokashi guide runs the whole branch, including what the buried batch has to do next.

Plan three: bring the worms in. A garage or basement worm bin at 50–70 °F composts at near-normal speed all winter; feeding slows a little below about 55 °F, but the system keeps eating your kitchen. Feed-rate numbers and the smell troubleshooting table live in the vermicomposting guide.

Pile care, November through February

  • Chop finer before the deep cold arrives. Microbes eat surfaces, and cold slows them twice: once by temperature, once by access. Shredded material that a warm pile finishes in weeks is the same material a cold pile can still work in a season.
  • Lean extra brown. Winter is wet — rain, melt, and snow loading into every open pile. Extra shredded cardboard, leaves, and straw buffer the soak and keep air channels open.
  • Cover it, but not sealed. Drape a tarp over the top with gaps at the sides, or roof the pile with a thick layer of straw. A fully sealed plastic wrap over a wet pile goes anaerobic under the snow and announces itself the week it thaws.
  • Turn on warm afternoons, and less. Every winter turn dumps core heat into cold air. If the core is hot, turn on the warmest day of the week — or skip it. A January pile reading 90 °F is not broken; it is winter doing winter things.
  • Read the temperature instead of guessing. A long-stem thermometer in the core tells you which game you’re playing — hot-core or cold-hold — and the thermometer showdown covers which one to buy.

The spring burst is real — plan for it

Dormant microbes rebound fast. When a winter pile’s core finally thaws and the first warm stretch arrives, activity doesn’t creep back — it surges, often visibly within days, fueled by a season of stored material and all the melt-water moisture. Expect the burst, and use it: the first spring job is a re-wet-and-turn (winter piles either dry out under covers or drown under melt), then fresh greens to restart the hot phase on schedule.

Harvest too: hot cores finish quietly through winter. Many piles deliver genuinely finished compost at the bottom by early spring — ambient, crumbly, nameable only as compost. The cure clock kept running while you weren’t looking; the two clocks in the timeline guide explain why spring is harvest season, not just build season.

Winter mistakes that actually kill piles

  • Turning a hot pile in a cold snap. You are venting its heat. Turn winter piles on warm afternoons only — the one habit that keeps January cores alive.
  • Dumping a frozen season’s worth of scraps and expecting winter to digest it. Winter stockpiles; spring devours. Plan the digestion step now (a hot pile’s edge, a spring trench, buried rows) instead of letting the backlog sit anaerobic in a cold bin.
  • Sealing a wet pile in plastic all winter. Airless plus soaked equals the rotten-egg smell that greets you at thaw. Covers shed water; they don’t suffocate.
  • Quitting collection because nothing is decomposing. Winter is when a household banks a year’s volume. Keep the caddy running and route it: stockpile, bokashi, indoor worms — three plans above.
  • Assuming the tumbler will carry the season. Uninsulated drums stall in freezing weather; buyer reports across our tumbler reviews write-ups show the pattern. In cold climates the drum is a spring-to-autumn machine, a storage bin in January, or — if insulated — something in between.

The questions, answered

Can you compost in winter?

Yes — everything keeps composting, just slower. A properly built hot pile can stay thermophilic all winter; cold bins, tumblers, and small piles slow toward pause; indoor systems (worms, bokashi, countertop digesters) barely notice the season. Collection continues at full speed regardless.

Does compost freeze?

Small and mid-size piles do freeze through, and the material goes dormant rather than dying. Piles of roughly a cubic metre or more with good mixing can keep a hot core even when the shell is frozen — you can dig snow off a working pile and find 100 °F underneath.

Do compost tumblers work in winter?

Barely, unless insulated. A plastic drum holding a small mass of material sheds heat faster than microbes can replace it, and activity near-stops in freezing weather. Tumblers earn their keep as storage drums in winter — or as insulated drums (double-wall models) if your climate is the constraint.

Should I stop collecting kitchen scraps in winter?

No. You bank volume from November through February — the stockpile mound, bokashi bucket, or indoor worm bin decides where it goes. Every winter of collected scraps is a head start on the spring pile.

When do I restart my pile in spring?

When temperatures hold consistently above about 40–50 °F: re-wet the pile, work in fresh greens, restart the turning cadence, and expect the thaw surge to finish what winter stored up.
