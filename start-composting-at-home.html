Beginner guide · B·01

How to start composting at home.

Most beginner compost advice is folklore dressed up as instruction. This is the version built on the numbers: pick the method your space and patience can actually support, feed the pile in the right proportions, give it water and air, and then get out of the way while the microbes do the work they already know how to do.

Pick the method before you buy anything

Composting is one biological process wearing five setups. The method decides your speed, your smell risk, and how much physical work you sign up for — so choose it against your actual life, not an idealized weekend.

If you have a yard: start with a bin (cold) or a tumbler, and graduate to a hot pile once leaves are abundant. If you have an apartment: worms first, bokashi second — the apartment guide covers that whole branch.

The six steps

  1. Set up a kitchen collection vessel. A small lidded pail or a bucket with a lid, emptied every 2–3 days. Lids are what keep fruit flies at zero. Many people keep scraps in the freezer between collections — it halts decomposition and smell entirely, which is fine: compost doesn’t care how the material spent its week off.
  2. Stockpile browns before you need them. The single most common beginner failure is running out of carbon mid-process. Fill a bag or bin now: shredded cardboard, leaves, small twigs, newspaper. This is the cheapest fuel in the whole system and it’s free in October.
  3. Build in layers at roughly 1 part wet scraps to 2–3 parts browns by volume. That ratio lands the mix near the 25:1–30:1 carbon-to-nitrogen band where microbes work fastest. Every dumping gets covered with browns — the cover is also what stops odor and flies.
  4. Water it to a wrung-out sponge. Target 50–60% moisture: squeeze a handful and a drop or two should appear, no stream. Too dry stalls the pile; too wet drives it anaerobic and it smells like a swamp.
  5. Decide your cadence: leave it or work it. A cold bin needs nothing but patience. A hot pile gets turned when the center cools below about 120 °F, which restarts the hot phase for another round. A tumbler gets a few spins after each load.
  6. Test for finished, don’t hope for finished. Done means: ambient temperature, crumbly texture, recognizable only as compost, no visible original material except the occasional corn cob or avocado pit. If you can still name what went in, it’s still cooking.

Greens and browns, actually

Greens (nitrogen, wet, fast): fruit and vegetable scraps, coffee grounds, tea leaves, fresh grass clippings in thin layers, plant-based food waste. Browns (carbon, dry, slow): dry leaves, shredded cardboard and newspaper, straw, small twigs, sawdust from untreated wood in small amounts. Eggshells are neither — they’s grit, add them freely, they do nothing chemically except break down very slowly.

The mental shortcut: every handful of kitchen scraps wants two to three handfuls of browns on top of it. That single habit prevents most smells, most sogginess, and most “why does my bin stink” emergencies.

What happens in the first 30 days

A new pile doesn’t decompose — it gets colonized, in order. Days 0–3: mesophilic microbes ignite and the mass begins to warm. Days 3–10 (if the mix and size are right): thermophiles take over and the center hits 120–160 °F. Days 10–30: the easy material is gone, the pile cools, and fungi and slower organisms move in to finish the job. This succession is the real engine of composting; article 02 covers who does what.

Two smells tell you where you are in the story and what to fix. Ammonia means too much nitrogen — add browns. Rotten eggs means airless — turn it and add coarse browns. The fix-it guide keys the whole set.

Five beginner mistakes that actually stall piles

  • All greens, no browns. A pure-scrap bin goes anaerobic and smelly, not composty. Browns are not filler; they are structure, air, and carbon.
  • A pile too small to heat. Self-heating is insulation physics: anything under roughly a cubic metre loses heat faster than the microbes make it. Small piles compost fine — just slowly, and cold.
  • Whole, unshredded material. Microbes eat surfaces. A whole cabbage takes a season; the same cabbage shredded takes weeks. Run the mower over leaf piles; tear cardboard into strips.
  • Turning on a schedule instead of on temperature. Turn when the pile starts to cool, or don’t turn at all. Turning a cold, cured pile achieves nothing.
  • Treating “cool again” as “finished.” A pile that has cooled is mid-process, not done. Curing — the weeks where the mix stabilizes — is where finished quality is actually decided.
