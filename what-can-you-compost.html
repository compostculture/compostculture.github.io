What can (and can’t) go in a compost bin.

There is no single yes/no list for composting — the same item is food in one system and contamination in another. The dividing line is how each method works: open aerobic decay, a hot thermophilic run, a sealed ferment, or a colony of sensitive animals. Here is the whole decision, one table.

November 2026 · 6 min read · four methods, one table

Why the rules change by method

Four systems, four physiologies. A hot pile held at 131–170 °F for three days sanitizes material an open bin would turn into a pest summit — which is why meat, dairy, and seed-bearing weeds belong only there. A cold bin sits near ambient temperature forever: it will decompose almost anything eventually, but anything protein-rich stays edible to rats the whole time. A worm bin is a confined livestock operation — the worms tolerate a plant-based diet, moderate moisture, and not much else; meat in a worm bin is a smell event, not a feeding event. Bokashi is anaerobic fermentation under bran and pressure: it accepts meat, dairy, and cooked food precisely because fermentation, not decay, is doing the work — and because its output gets buried to finish, not left in an open bin.

“Yes” = expected, routine, no management needed. “Small amounts” = fine mixed in, not as a habit. “No” = it causes pests, odor, pathogens, or plastic contamination in that system. Bioplastics fail everywhere at home for the same reason: materials like PLA need sustained industrial-composter temperatures (>140 °F) in a facility, not a backyard cycle — check your municipal collection rules instead.

The never list, and what it actually protects

  • Pet waste and litter. The concern is pathogens — Toxoplasma gondii in cat feces, Baylisascaris in dog feces — that a home hot pile cannot be relied on to reach the time-temperature combination to neutralize. Composting toilets exist; backyard bins don’t cut it.
  • Meat and dairy in open, cool bins. It’s not heresy, it’s logistics: at ambient temperature a meat scrap stays a rodent dinner for months. In a real hot core or a bokashi ferment, the same scrap is handled before pests can stage there.
  • Glossy paper and “compostable” plastics. Coatings are plastics; PLA needs conditions no home system provides. Contaminated finished compost is still contaminated compost.
  • Treated wood. The whole point of treatment is that biology doesn’t eat it. Untreated sawdust in small amounts is fine; anything painted, sealed, or pressure-treated is not compost, anywhere.

Quantity is part of the answer

Even approved materials have rates. A worm colony eats roughly 25–50% of its body weight per day — a bin stocked with 5 lb of worms tops out near 1–2 lb of scraps a day; overfeeding an undersized bin is the single most common indoor failure (the sign is a sour or ammonia smell and food sitting visibly unchanged). Bokashi is the opposite discipline: layer scraps with bran and press the air out each time, because it’s the anaerobic ferment, not the volume, that does the work. Cold bins are the most forgiving system alive — their failure mode is not disaster but months of nothing, which usually means the mix is all wet greens and no structure.

The rate numbers for a working worm bin — feeding schedule, moisture, temperature band — are laid out in the vermicomposting guide; the bokashi bucket mechanics are in the bokashi setup page.
