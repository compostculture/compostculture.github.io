How we pick a kitchen caddy

A caddy does not compost anything. It buys you three or four days of patience between the counter and wherever the scraps actually go — worm bin, bokashi bucket, stockpile, curbside bin. So we judge on the things that protect that patience:

  • The seal and the odor story. A lid that actually closes, and a carbon filter or engineered vent — the difference between a pail nobody notices and a fruit-fly magnet. Every pail below states which mechanism it uses.
  • Capacity against your emptying cadence. This site’s reference point — a 5-gallon bucket holds roughly a week of scraps for a two-person household — puts the category-standard 1–1.3-gallon pails at roughly two to three days each. The 1.75-gallon OXO buys headroom for a cooking-heavy week.
  • Cleanup. A caddy that’s annoying to wash gets ignored, and an ignored caddy becomes the bag under the sink. Dishwasher-safe bodies and wide openings get weight here.
  • Where it lives. Counter, wall, under-sink, or docked to the trash — the pick that stays out of the way is the pick that keeps getting emptied.

What a caddy is for — and what it isn’t

  • It buys time, not decomposition. Everything on this page is collection hardware. The processing decision is a separate one — a worm bin, a bokashi bucket, a pile, or a curbside bin — and the caddy’s only job is to make the trip from counter to system routine.
  • Size to your emptying cadence, not your ambition. The standard 1–1.3-gallon pail is roughly two to three days of a two-person kitchen by this site’s volume math. A pail that sits full for a week in July is a fruit-fly incubator no matter how good its filter is; the winter guide’s stockpile plan works because the caddy gets emptied every few days.
  • Bags or bare is a real fork. Liners (simplehuman’s Code Z roll, AIRNEX’s 20 bags, the bags-tuck-inside OXO scheme) make carry-out clean; bare pails create no wrapper waste but demand a rinse. Pick based on which chore you’ll actually repeat.
  • Filters are consumables — budget them. The charcoal-filter pails (Epica, Utopia, RED FACTOR, KAZAI, AIRNEX, the under-sink pail) work while the filter works. The Epica listing’s 3–6 month replacement cadence is the honest planning number.
  • Warm weather changes the answer. A vented-lid pick that’s fine in January is the one to re-think in July. If a kitchen pail starts smelling, the diagnosis table is on the troubleshooting page.

What we skipped

  • Generic bokashi bucket kits. We verified a 20-liter bokashi bucket-and-bran kit ($69.99, 36 ratings) hiding in caddy searches. It’s a bokashi system, not a caddy — and bokashi deserves the picks with the field data and the method behind them: they’re on the bokashi kits page.
  • “Compost lockers.” The phrase mostly returns outdoor locking bins and small tumblers on Amazon. For countertop collection, the pails above are the indoor answer; for outdoor storage, the bins page already ranks the real lockers.
