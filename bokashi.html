Bokashi composting: how the method works — step by step.

Bokashi is not a slow pile. It's a fast lactic-acid fermentation that turns kitchen waste — including the meat, dairy, and cooked food most compost methods reject — into pre-compost in about two weeks, at room temperature, in a sealed bucket on your counter. This page is the method: the science, the batch steps, the rules that beat the hardware, and what to look for in a kit. The buying decision — SCD, TeraGanix, bran top-ups, the DIY route — lives on our Best Bokashi Kits page.

What to look for in a bokashi kit

A bokashi bucket is only as good as its seal — the fermentation is anaerobic, so air that leaks in is the failure mode that turns a two-week pre-compost into a two-week smell. Four things decide a kit, in order of how much they matter:

  • The seal. Lid, gasket, latch — the hardware that keeps oxygen out decides whether the method works at all, long before looks or brand.
  • What's in the box. A true starter kit ships the effective microorganisms and the bran; a bare bucket-and-bran deal means sourcing the culture yourself — cheaper, with one more thing to measure.
  • One bin or two. Single-bucket systems pause between batches while you bury a batch; a two-bin kit ferments continuously. If a household cooks daily, the pause is the first thing you'll want to fix.
  • Drain and capacity. A spigot with a strainer makes tea removal routine; a 5-gallon bucket takes roughly a week of scraps for a two-person household.

How bokashi actually works (so the gear makes sense)

  • It's fermentation, not decomposition. A culture of effective microorganisms — lactobacilli and yeasts seeded onto wheat bran — ferments the waste the way sauerkraut ferments: the bucket's pH drops to roughly 3.5–4, which is acidic enough that the rot you'd normally fear (anaerobic pathogens) can't thrive. That's the entire reason meat and dairy are allowed in a bokashi bucket and not in a hot pile.
  • There's no heat phase. The bucket works at room temperature. The 131°F pathogen and weed-seed kill that defines a hot pile doesn't happen in the bucket — it happens later, when the fermented material goes into soil or a live pile. Bokashi output is pre-compost, not finished compost.
  • Bran is the only consumable. About a quarter cup of bran per inch of waste, every layer. It carries the culture and supplies the carbon the lactobacilli feed on. When the bucket runs out of bran, the method stops — which is why the top-up in this comparison is its own pick.
  • Watch the tea and the mold. A dark liquid ("bokashi tea") drains to the spigot as things ferment — that's normal. A pale tan or white mold (koji) on the surface is a healthy sign. Black mold or a putrid smell means air got in and the batch failed.

One batch, start to finish

  • Load in thin layers. Scraps go in as your kitchen produces them, with a quarter cup of dry bran over every inch of waste. No bran under a layer means that layer rots.
  • Press out the air. Compact each layer as you go — the anaerobic seal starts inside the mass, not at the lid. This is the difference between koji and failure.
  • Close it after every addition. Filling a 5-gallon bucket takes two to five days of normal cooking; the lid stays sealed the whole time.
  • Drain the tea. Once the spigot starts dripping, open it a few times a week. Dilute roughly 1:100 for plants or skip it — it's a byproduct, not a required input.
  • Ferment two to four weeks. Sealed, unopened, room temperature. Pale tan mold is the good kind; black mold or a putrid smell means air got in.
  • Bury, don't dump. The finished mass is pre-compost: at least two inches deep in soil or a trench, covered, and it finishes in place in roughly two weeks. Spring is burial season — see the spring guide.

Run the bucket right

  • Quarter cup of bran, every inch of waste. This is the whole method. A layer of scraps with no bran underneath is a layer that rots.
  • Press it down. Air pockets are what turn a batch black. Compacting each layer is the difference between koji and failure.
  • Drain the tea. When the spigot starts dripping, open it — a few times a week. Use it 1:100 diluted on houseplants, or don't use it at all; it's a byproduct, not a required input.
  • Know the mold. Pale tan or white = koji, the good kind. Black, or a putrid smell = air got in, and that batch goes to the pile, not the garden.
  • It's stage one. Bury fermented material at least two inches deep in soil (it finishes in about two weeks) or mix it into a live pile. Straight into a pot as if it were compost is how people get disappointed with bokashi.
