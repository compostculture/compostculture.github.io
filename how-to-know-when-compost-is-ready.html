Practical guide · Finish-it

How to know your compost is actually finished — four tests, the cure, and the winter trap.

Every compost guide hands you a timeline; none of them are a green light. What finished compost is, the jar, reheat, radish, and worm tests that extension services actually run, why “not hot” does not mean “done,” and what harvesting early costs you in the garden.

November 2026 · 11 min read · four tests, one trap

Ranges from Cornell CWMI, NC State, Colorado, Florida, and Wisconsin extension guides; the no-reheat benchmark is NC State’s (finished within 10 °F of ambient) and CSU’s (curing begins when a turned pile no longer climbs above 120 °F).

What finished actually looks like

Extension services describe the same end state in nearly identical words. Finished compost is:

  • Dark and crumbly. Brown to black, uniform, humus-like — it holds together loosely when squeezed and falls apart when broken, and feels moist like a damp sponge, never slimy.
  • Smells like soil. Pleasant and earthy, like forest duff. Ammonia, sour or vinegary notes, or rotten-egg smell mean decomposition is still running.
  • Unrecognizable. You cannot identify the original ingredients. The exceptions are real and allowed: wood chips, twigs, corn cobs, fruit pits, and eggshell fragments break down slowly, and extension guides still call a pile finished when only those survivors remain.
  • Shrunk. A finished pile is about half its starting volume — NC State says closer to a third. A bin that still looks like what you put in it isn’t done no matter how dark the top layer is.
  • Cool. At or near ambient temperature, and it stays there after turning.

How strictly you apply this depends on what the compost is for. Guides from Oregon State and the Institute for Local Self-Reliance are explicit that “finished” is end-use dependent: compost tilled into a field in fall can be cruder than compost you will start seeds in. The tests below are the proof for the stricter uses.

The four tests you can actually run at home

1. The jar test — stability by smell

Take a sample from the middle of the pile, not the crust. Moisten it until it feels damp but not soggy, seal it in a glass jar or a plastic bag with the air squeezed out, and leave it at room temperature. Cornell’s Compost Science for the Environment runs it for a full week at 20–30 °C; Wisconsin’s master-composter course uses five to seven days; several extensions accept “several days.” Open it and judge: earthy smell, no mold, no slime — stable, ready. Putrid, sour, ammonia-sharp, or a bag puffed with gas — microbes are still actively decomposing inside, starving for oxygen and making organic acids. That is an unfinished pile announcing itself.

2. The reheat test — stability by temperature

A finished pile is finished because its food is gone, and dead food doesn’t reheat. Pack a moist sample (roughly 40–50% moisture — wrung-sponge feel) into a wide-mouth jar or thermos, seal it, insulate the container, and leave it somewhere warm for two to three days. Then probe the center. If the sample sits more than a few degrees above room temperature, decomposition is still burning and the compost is not stable — Cornell’s laboratory version measures the same thing as CO2 production and draws the line at 10 mg CO2 per gram of organic carbon per day. A 20-inch compost thermometeraffiliate link is the right instrument: its stem reaches a pile core or a jar center without guessing, and it is the class of probe compared in the thermometer showdown.

3. The radish test — the garden votes

The lab standard is the germination index: soak filter paper in a compost-water extract, sprout garden cress seeds against a plain-water control, and score both germination and root length. A germination index of 80% or better is the published maturity line — 50–80% is the moderate-phytotoxicity band, and that is compost that will hurt seedlings. The extension home version is simpler and just as decisive. Florida’s simplest test: sow radish seeds directly in pots of your compost; if three-quarters sprout and grow into radishes, the compost is ready for any use. Their extract version: soak a quarter cup of compost in half a cup of water for two hours, strain it, and water fifteen seeds on damp tissue with a few drops of the liquid against a water control in the dark at about 27 °C; germination at 80% of the control rate is satisfactory, and markedly worse means immature. Use radish or cress — tomato is famously resilient and makes a bad test seed.

4. The worm signal — the pile grades itself

During curing, as pH stabilizes and the chemistry mellows, invertebrates move in. Cornell-area guides count red wigglers, millipedes, and sow bugs colonizing the curing zone as a readiness signal; Michigan State’s vermicomposting guides treat cocoons and newly hatched worms of mixed ages as evidence of a healthy finished environment. One honesty note: the popular home recipe — drop worms in and watch them flee — is a laboratory method, not a garage one. Formal earthworm avoidance tests run in sealed two-chamber choice boxes under ISO protocol. In your yard, presence means ready; absence in a closed bin means nothing at all.

Why “not hot” isn’t “done”: the curing phase

The most common mistake in composting is reading the cooling of a pile as the end of the process. Heat stops because the easily digestible food is gone; what remains is slower material and a microbial community that has to change over — fungi and slower-growing organisms doing the final breakdown at their own pace. That is the cure, and extension timelines agree it is where finish quality is decided: roughly 30 days minimum (NC State’s organic-farms guide), 4–6 weeks (Oregon State), 4–8 weeks (Nevada), 6–12 weeks (NC State’s Gardener’s Handbook), 2–3 months (Washington State, which calls material less than three months old unripe), and Colorado’s clean rule of at least 45 days after the pile’s last reheat. Florida allows the full honest range: a month to over a year.

The hot composting guide ends at exactly this point for a reason: most people stop the moment the core drops below 100 °F, sift, and spread compost that is dark but not finished. Skipping the cure is how half-finished fragments end up in your beds — and the costs are section-size, not trivial.

What using it too early actually costs

  1. Nitrogen lock-up. Oklahoma State calls this “perhaps the most harmful result of using immature compost”: unfinished material still high in carbon recruits soil microbes that scavenve plant-available nitrogen to keep decomposing, and your plants yellow and stunt while the pile finishes itself in your garden bed. Colorado State says the same — unfinished compost with a high carbon ratio scavenges nitrogen from the soil. Massachusetts extension’s countermeasure is a waiting rule: give applied compost about a week before you seed or transplant into it.
  2. Phytotoxicity. Immature compost contains ammonia, acetic and other organic acids, and phenolic compounds that inhibit germination, shorten roots, and kill young seedlings outright. This is precisely what the radish test detects — and precisely why extension guides separate “finished for field mulch” from “ready for pots.”
  3. Ammonium burn. Compost made from nitrogen-rich feedstocks that hasn’t cured is often high in ammonium, which burns roots under a mulch and leaves in a top-dressing. Vermont extension flags the same chemistry in potting mixes: ammonia and undecomposed organic acids damage germinating seeds and young transplants.
  4. Reheating where your roots are. Unstable compost kept moist keeps decomposing, and decomposition consumes oxygen. Cornell’s framing is blunt: unstable compost “uses the nitrate and oxygen needed by plants.” Compost still in its hot phase, buried as mulch or top-dressing, can reheat — and a reheating layer against tender roots and seedlings cooks them.

There is a fifth, quieter risk. The sanitation event only happens if the pile actually ran hot: 131 °F (55 °C) held three consecutive days is the USDA benchmark this site uses throughout (see the hot composting guide), and sustained heat above 140 °F is what kills most weed seeds. A pile harvested “early because it looks dark” may never have completed that window — and immature compost can reintroduce weed seeds and pathogens into the exact garden you composted to feed.

When to start testing — and the winter trap

Use the floor of the timeline, not the ceiling. The timeline guide puts a properly managed hot pile at 1–3 months, batch tumblers at 2–5 weeks per batch, and a turned-when-remembered cold pile at 3–12 months. Start running the tests at the low end of your method’s range: the jar test costs a jar, and the reheat test costs a weekend. Worm bins don’t finish in this sense at all — vermicomposting is a continuous harvest, and the worm guide covers when trays read ready.

The trap is winter. A cold, quiet, dark bin in February looks finished by every visual sign — and it is usually just dormant. Microbial rates collapse in the cold, half-processed material sits suspended, and what looks like finished compost thaws into an anaerobic mess in March. The spring guide’s harvest test is the right one for thawed material — room-temperature, crumbly, unrecognizable — and the winter guide explains what the cold actually did to your pile. If your “finished” pile spent the winter frozen, re-run the jar or reheat test after the spring surge, before you trust it.

Screen it, then store it

The last step is the one in the photograph above: screening. A simple mesh — hardware cloth, a store-bought compost screen (the field, pick by pick, in the sifter roundup), or a rack of window screen stapled over a frame — separates the finished material from the overs. The overs go back into the next pile, exactly where they belong; the hot pile’s cure section calls that residue inoculant, not waste, because it carries the living community your new build needs.

What comes through the screen is stable, and stability is storage: properly finished compost doesn’t spoil or “go off.” Keep it covered so it doesn’t dry to powder or wash out in a storm, and it stays usable indefinitely. If it dries out and goes quiet, that’s not spoilage either — rewetting reactivates it, which is the whole premise of compost tea.

Questions the tests raise

My compost smells. Is it ready?

A finished pile smells like a forest floor — pleasant, earthy, like soil. Ammonia, sour vinegar or rancid-butter notes, or rotten-egg smell mean decomposition is still running: not ready. The jar test is the tiebreaker: seal a moist sample for several days and judge the odor when you open it.

How long should compost cure?

After the pile stops reheating, extension recommendations cluster at 4–12 weeks: about 30 days (NC State), 4–6 weeks (Oregon State), 4–8 weeks (Nevada), 6–12 weeks (NC State Gardener’s Handbook), 2–3 months (Washington State). Colorado adds a clean rule: cure at least 45 days after the last reheat. Cold climates run slower.

Why are wood chips and corn cobs still visible?

Lignin breaks down slowly. Wood chips, twigs, corn cobs, fruit pits, and eggshell fragments legitimately survive even a well-run pile, and extension guides still call that compost finished. Screen them out and return them to the next pile as inoculant — survivors are not evidence of an unfinished batch.

Can I use half-finished compost at all?

Yes, for forgiving uses: tilled into empty beds in fall or top-dressed around established plantings, it keeps decomposing harmlessly in place. No for seeds, seedlings, and potting mixes — that is where nitrogen lock-up and phytotoxicity bite. Maturity requirements are set by how you intend to use the compost.

My pile reheats when I turn it. Is something wrong?

During the build, no — a temperature bounce after each turn is proof the hot phase is working. But a pile you believe is cured that clearly reheats above air temperature after being wetted and turned was not finished: let it sit another few weeks and run the reheat test.
