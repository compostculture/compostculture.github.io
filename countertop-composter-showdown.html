Countertop composters, head-to-head: six machines, three kinds

“Countertop composter” is one label covering three different technologies. A $299 dehydrator and a $900 bio-reactor both fit on a kitchen counter and both “compost” your scraps — by completely different chemistry, with different costs after the sticker. This page runs the six machines we track against each other on the numbers that decide which one you actually need.

The real decisionbatch vs. continuous, and what you’ll pay after the sticker

One line: the batch machines — dehydrators and microbial drums alike — work through your food in cycles; a bio-reactor runs a living ecosystem that digests it continuously. Match the machine to your household size and your meat — then check the three-year cost.

Three kinds of machines wearing one label

Open any shopping site and the same category lists machines that do three different jobs. The split matters because the chemistry decides what goes in, what comes out, and what you’re still paying two years later.

Camp 1 — batch dehydrator with a consumable

The Lomi 1 heats a small drum, bakes out the water, grinds the residue, and pairs the drum with replaceable pods you buy per cycle. It’s the category’s best-known machine, and the one whose real cost shows up on the consumable line.

Camp 2 — the batch drums: dehydrators and the microbial outlier

The Ouaken and Growell bake out the water without a per-cycle cartridge; the Reencle Prime is the drum in this camp that doesn’t dehydrate at all — a microbial colony digests each batch in 2–24-hour cycles. Reencle’s purification uses a replaceable carbon filter at long intervals rather than a per-cycle pod; Ouaken and Growell publish no recurring consumable at all. Bigger drums than the Lomi (4–14 L) and lower sticker prices.

Camp 3 — continuous bio-reactor

The GEME Pro and GEME Terra 2 run a live, self-replicating microbial ecosystem under GEME’s GEMEBrain adaptive control, with a permanent Metal Ion Oxidation purification system. They take meat, dairy, and small bones on the same continuous feed as peels, run 24/7 instead of in batches, and produce a moist active compost substrate rather than a dried flake. They’re also the most expensive machines here — and the ones with the longest cleaning cadence.

The skip list — when the right machine is none of them

Skip all of this if you have a yard

A managed yard pile is still the cheapest tonne of compost on earth, and for a property with space and a season of turning, every machine on this page is a convenience you don’t need. The bin comparison covers that side of the fence.

Skip the Lomi if you want meat in

The drum’s exclusions list (meat, dairy, pizza crusts) and the pod subscription are the two things it’s known for. Both are fine if your scraps are produce-and-grounds and you’re comfortable with per-cycle cost.

Skip the Reencle if you want it running nonstop

It works in 2–24-hour microbial batches and the drum empties every 1–3 months — the best drum-per-dollar for a two-person household that cooks regularly, but if you want the machine always ready for the next load, a continuous bio-reactor (either GEME) is the design that does that. Meat is not the deciding factor: the maker lists meat, dairy, and even bones among its accepted inputs.

Skip the GEME machines if you want bagged output

Their output is a moist active substrate, not a dry, shelf-stable flake. If “finished compost” to you means a bag you can store in the garage, a dehydrator does that and a GEME doesn’t. And if the household is under three, the $699–$900 price is hard to justify against a $299 dehydrator.

The bottom line

For the most readers — a two-person household with regular cooking — the Reencle Prime is the buy: the largest drum in the batch camp at a fair price, with no per-cycle consumable to budget against. For anyone whose scraps include meat, the question isn’t which machine, it’s which GEME: the Terra 2 at $699 for 1–3 people, the Pro at $899.99 for 4+. And if the scraps are mostly produce and the budget is real, the $299 dehydrators earn their place. The questions, answered

What is the actual difference between a dehydrator and a microbial drum?

The chemistry, not the habit. Dehydrators (Lomi, Growell, Ouaken) apply heat and airflow to drive off the water. The Reencle Prime runs a microbial colony instead: each batch digests in 2–24-hour cycles and the output is a dry pre-compost rather than a dehydrated flake. The batch workflow is the same either way — load, run, empty.

Which machines take meat and dairy?

The three microbial machines. The Reencle Prime's maker lists meat, dairy, and even bones among its accepted inputs, and the GEME bio-reactors are marketed to take meat, dairy, and small bones on a continuous feed. The dehydrators do not: the Lomi publishes meat, dairy, and pizza crusts as exclusions, and the Growell and Ouaken publish per-model input lists.

Is the Lomi really the only one with a per-cycle consumable?

As listed, yes — with one nuance. The Lomi takes a pod per cycle. The Reencle Prime's only recurring part is a carbon filter with a maker-quoted life of roughly 9–12 months. The Ouaken and Growell publish no recurring consumable, and the GEMEs publish none at all. So the year-2 cost differences on this page are real, not hypothetical.

What should I budget beyond the sticker?

Lomi: pods, one per cycle. Reencle Prime: a carbon filter about a year in. Ouaken, Growell, GEMEs: nothing published. Energy is the only ongoing cost for all of them, and the two GEME bio-reactors are the only machines that run continuously.
